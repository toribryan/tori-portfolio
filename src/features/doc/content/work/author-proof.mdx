## Where it sits

Proctorio's integrity suite is three products. Integrity proctors the exam,
Vault protects the exam content, and Origin proves authorship. I led
cross-product design across all three, for institutions and for proctoring
agencies. AuthorProof is Origin's product, and the one I took from an assigned
brief to a shippable MVP.

<ProctorioSuites caption="The three suites. Hover one to see it move." />

## Did you write this?

Existing integrity tools answer one question: is this original? A paraphrase,
an AI rewrite, or a ghostwriter pass clears every one of them. None answer the
question instructors actually have: did you write this?

<IntegrityRadar caption="What the suite has to cover. Authorship, in green, was the vector no existing tool touched." />

AuthorProof asks that question directly. When a student submits written work,
it generates a short comprehension quiz from the student's own writing, in the
submission's own language. Someone who wrote the paper can answer questions
about it. Someone who didn't, can't. The result surfaces as a score badge beside
the submission, where instructors already grade, with a link out to the full
session for anyone who wants the question-level detail.

## The brief as it arrived

The product came with its architecture already chosen: three journeys built on
the LMS's own quiz tooling, one quiz per submission, with the student notified
to go find it.

<BriefedJourneys caption="The first concept as it was handed over: the instructor configures, the student submits and answers, the instructor grades." />

Before engineering committed time to it, I mapped that flow step by step and
timed it against what the platform could actually do.

<ConstraintStats />

## Four constraints, each a design problem in disguise

- **The quiz tooling is one-to-many.** It assumes a quiz is authored once and
  assigned to a class. A fresh quiz per submission fights that assumption at
  every step, and gets fragile when a whole class submits at once.
- **Ten minutes is the whole attack window.** Fetch, generate, create, assign,
  notify added up to roughly ten minutes between submission and quiz. That gap
  is exactly the time a student needs to pull their essay back up, so the
  architecture was working against the one thing the product had to prove.
- **Students turn notifications off.** The inbox is rarely checked, and email
  timing varies by institution. The fallback would have been "instructors
  remind students," which puts the burden on the exact person we were trying
  to help.
- **Two disconnected things to grade.** A separate quiz item meant the
  submission and the quiz would live in different places, with no unified view
  for the instructor.

Each one changes where the product should live, not how a screen looks. That
is why I call them design problems, and why the answer was a different flow
rather than a workaround for each.

## A smaller flow that kept the promise

Rather than report the blockers, I pitched an alternative and split it into an
MVP and a longer vision. The MVP embeds AuthorProof in the assignment itself:
the student starts the assignment, uploads their essay, and the quiz appears
seconds later.

<PivotFlows caption="The student's path as it was briefed, and as it was pitched instead." />

That one move collapsed the timing gap, removed the notification dependency,
and stepped out of the quiz tooling entirely. The result is written back beside
the submission, so the instructor has one thing to read.

I was also clear about what the pitch cost. It excluded short pieces written
directly in the LMS editor, and it left an open question about how proctoring
would coexist with the embedded flow. I would rather flag the hole myself than
have engineering find it. The longer-vision pieces were parked on a roadmap
rather than dropped, and that direction was later adopted as the product's
official long-term vision, with the briefed idea shipping first as the proof
of concept.

## Confidence, not verdict

The result had to read as evidence for a conversation between an instructor
and a student, never as an automated judgment. Three rules followed from that:

- **No binary pass or fail.** A score ships with a confidence level, and the
  instructor decides what to do with it.
- **Transparency into what was evaluated.** Every generated question sits
  beside the student's own words that prompted it and beside the assessment of
  the answer, so a score traces back to something a person can read.
- **Never color alone.** The host platform controls the badge color and maps it
  the opposite way from the score, so the score, label, and confidence are
  always carried as text.

## The prototype was the spec

I led design end to end on a four-to-six-week timeline from concept to
production-ready MVP. I designed the settings model for quiz configuration and
built its interface, designed the report card, the quiz results, and the emails
students receive, and built the front end for all of it in HTML, CSS, and
vanilla JavaScript on our design system. Engineering got working prototypes
rather than a document about them.

In the first-semester pilot, 95% of test takers completed their quiz, the
false-positive rate stayed under 5%, and customer feedback on the concept was
sweepingly positive.

## What I'd do next

- Prototype the embedded flow alongside a proctored session earlier, so that
  open question gets resolved before the direction decision instead of after.
- Let instructors review generated questions before they reach a student.
- A per-language quiz picker, beyond the submission-language default.
