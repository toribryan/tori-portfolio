Most senior living sites meet a family with a lead form and a stock photo.
That's the whole first impression, at the exact moment someone is scared and in
a hurry.

Modern Care Homes is the version that doesn't do that. Families across the
Phoenix metro search independent and assisted living homes, compare what they
find, and contact a home directly. I'm the design engineer on it, which means I
design the interface and then build the front end that ships it.

Let's get into why the hard part isn't the search.

## Two people, one search

The thing that makes this a real design problem is that two very different
people use it for the same task.

There's the resident, usually over 70, on a tablet or a desktop zoomed way in,
trying to work out whether a place looks like somewhere they could actually
live. And there's their adult child, on a phone, in the middle of a stressful
day, trying to compare six homes and find cost and care level fast.

They have almost nothing in common. Neither one has any patience for an
interface that gets in the way.

The flow is search, then compare, then contact. Compare is where people get
stuck. So compare is where the work goes.

Senior living is a marketplace problem before it's a healthcare one. That one
decision settles a lot, because it means the products worth studying aren't
other care sites. They're the marketplaces that already spent years and real
money getting search and comparison right.

So that's where I went. I spend a lot of time in <Tech name="mobbin" /> pulling
apart how real estate marketplaces handle discovery. Filters, result density,
map and list views, what a card shows at a glance. Most of it transfers. Some
of it doesn't, because our filters are care levels instead of bedrooms, and the
person deciding often isn't the person moving in.

The agent side got its own teardown. Listing a home is a long form with photos,
pricing and details, and the best version of that problem anywhere is Airbnb's
listing flow. I pulled it apart screen by screen. What someone is doing, seeing
and thinking at each stage, then what works and where the opening is.

That teardown paid for the two biggest changes in the whole project. Here they
are.

## The home card

A family is mostly trying to rule homes out. The card should help them do that.

<Compare
  before="/case-studies/old-homecard-mch.png"
  after="/case-studies/new-homecard-mch.png"
  beforeAlt="The old home card: photo, price above the name in grey, address, and a paragraph of generic marketing copy"
  afterAlt="The reworked home card: an Available Now badge, a save control, photo carousel dots, then name, address and price"
  layout="columns"
  caption="The home card, before and after."
/>

The old one gave its widest line to marketing copy that read identically on
every listing. When you're comparing six homes, identical copy is worse than
nothing. It's noise sitting exactly where the useful information should be.

The new card spends that space on what actually differs between homes. Whether
it's available now. That there's more than one photo. A way to save it for
later. Nothing on the card is there unless it helps you tell this home apart
from the next one.

Underneath that is a quieter job: content design. Senior living is full of
jargon, and most families are learning it while they shop. Independent living,
assisted living, levels of care. If the words on the page assume you already
know the difference, the page failed.

## The agent dashboard

The old dashboard was two nav items and a blank canvas. Which tells an agent
nothing about the listing they're supposed to be finishing.

<Compare
  before="/case-studies/old-dashboard-mch.png"
  after="/case-studies/new-dashboard-mch.png"
  beforeAlt="The old agent dashboard shell: a wide sidebar holding only Home Details and Settings, next to an empty content area"
  afterAlt="The reworked shell: an icon rail, then a column listing Availability, Photos, About, Amenities, Care services and Contact, each with its current state underneath"
  layout="columns"
  caption="The agent dashboard shell, before and after."
/>

The new one turns the sidebar into the listing itself. Every section
(Availability, Photos, About, Amenities, Care services, Contact) carries its
own state, so what's thin is visible before you open anything.

That's the Airbnb move, lifted straight from the teardown. Show the state of
every stage, so the person can see what's left. Same idea, care listing instead
of a rental. Promise fulfilled.

## The part nobody screenshots

Filters. No results. Listings with half their fields blank.

These are most of the real sessions and none of the pretty pictures, and they
get designed like they matter. Because they do. A directory that only looks
good when every field is filled in isn't finished. It's a demo.

## One system, used twice

Everything above has to survive being used in two places. The same components
run on the public marketplace and inside the agent platform. A change to a
button is a change in two products.

That's the job the design system does, and it's where I spend a large share of
my time.

The accessibility floor lives inside the components, not in a checklist
somewhere. 18px base type, 48-by-48 targets, AA contrast as the minimum.
Because it's baked into the component, I can't quietly undo it on page eleven.
That's what designing _from_ a floor rather than _toward_ one actually means.

<Tech name="storybook" /> is where components get built and argued with before
they go near a page. Every state, every size, at 200% zoom, failing on their
own instead of failing inside a layout. It's also how I know a component is
ready for both products. I can look at it outside of either one.

## I ship what I design

This is the part that removes the most steps.

Components, layout, responsive behavior and motion all leave as code from me,
not as a spec I hand to someone else. Accessibility gets checked on the real
build (contrast, focus order, keyboard paths, zoom, target sizes) because a
mockup can't tell you what happens at 200 percent. And whatever I learn
shipping goes back into the system, so the next screen starts further along
than the last one.

The part I like most: I never have to defend any of it in a handoff meeting. If
something needs to change, I go change it.

## One quick caveat

None of this is finished, and I'd rather say so.

Foundations are locked. Components are still in progress, publishing as they
get done. Comparison isn't solved yet. Cost and care level side by side, which
is the actual decision families are making, and it's still open. It's the next
real problem, not a solved one I'm dressing up.

That's the honest state of it. A live product, shipping, with the hardest
screen still ahead.
