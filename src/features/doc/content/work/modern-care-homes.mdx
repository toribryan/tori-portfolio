## Two people, one search

Modern Care Homes is a senior living platform for the Phoenix metro. Families
search independent living and assisted living homes across Phoenix, Scottsdale,
Peoria, Paradise Valley, Queen Creek and Surprise, compare what they find, and
contact a home directly.

<Video
    src="/case-studies/home-grid-mch.mp4"
    caption="Browsing homes: the comparison grid families land on after a search."
/>

What makes it a hard design problem is that two very different people use it
for the same task. There's the resident, usually over 70, on a tablet or a
desktop with the browser zoomed way in, trying to figure out whether a place
looks like somewhere they could live. And there's their adult child, on a
phone, often in the middle of something stressful, trying to compare six homes
and find cost and care level fast.

They don't have much in common except that neither one has any patience for an
interface that gets in the way. Nearly every decision I've made on this project
comes back to that.

And they're both stuck in the same place. The core flow is search, then
compare, then contact, and compare is where people actually get held up.
Ruling homes out fast is the whole job, so that's where the work goes.

Senior living is a marketplace problem before it's a healthcare one. That
framing decides a lot, because it means the products worth studying aren't other
care sites. They're the marketplaces that already spent years and a lot of money
on search, comparison and listing quality.

I spend a lot of time in <Tech name="mobbin" /> pulling apart how real estate
marketplaces handle search and discovery: filters, result density, map and
list views, saved comparisons, what a card shows at a glance. Most of what those
products landed on carries over. Some of it doesn't, because our filters are
care levels rather than bedrooms and bathrooms, and the person deciding often
isn't the person moving in.

The agent side has its own teardown. Getting a home listed is a long form with
photos, pricing and details, and the closest good version of that problem is
Airbnb's listing creation flow. I pulled it apart screen by screen and mapped
what someone is doing, seeing and thinking at each stage, then marked what
works and where the openings are. It's a teardown I keep coming back to,
because listing creation is the same problem in every marketplace I work on.
It's also where the biggest change on the agent platform came from, below.

## Two reframes the research paid for

Two kinds of design sit next to each other here and they don't always want the
same thing. Product design decides whether someone can finish what they came to
do. Web design decides whether they trust the place enough to keep going. The
research turned into one concrete change on each side.

### The home card should spend its space on what differs

A family is mostly trying to rule homes out. So a listing should lead with what
actually separates one home from the next, like care level, setting, cost and
what's included, not copy that reads the same on every page.

<Compare

  after="/case-studies/new-homecard-mch.png"
  beforeAlt="The old home card: photo, price above the name in grey, address, and a paragraph of generic marketing copy"
  afterAlt="The reworked home card: an Available Now badge, a save control, photo carousel dots, then name, address and price"
  layout="columns"
  caption="The home card, before and after."
/>

The old card gave its widest line to marketing copy that read identically on
every listing, which is no help when you're comparing. The new one spends that
space on what differs between homes: whether it's available, more than one
photo, and a way to keep it for later.

There's a content-design job underneath this too. Senior living has a lot of
jargon and most families are learning it while they shop: independent living,
assisted living, levels of care. If the words on the page assume you already
know the difference, the page failed.

### The agent dashboard should _be_ the listing

<Compare
  before="/case-studies/old-dashboard-mch.png"
  after="/case-studies/new-dashboard-mch.png"
  beforeAlt="The old agent dashboard shell: a wide sidebar holding only Home Details and Settings, next to an empty content area"
  afterAlt="The reworked shell: an icon rail, then a column listing Availability, Photos, About, Amenities, Care services and Contact, each with its current state underneath"
  layout="columns"
  caption="The agent dashboard shell, before and after."
/>

The old shell was two nav items and a blank canvas, which tells an agent
nothing about the listing they're supposed to be finishing. Now the sidebar is
the listing. Every section, from Availability and Photos through About,
Amenities, Care services and Contact, carries its own state, so what's thin is
visible before you open anything. That idea came straight out of the Airbnb
teardown: the same "show the state of every stage" move, applied to a care
listing instead of a rental.

Around both of these sit the unglamorous states: filters, no results, listings
with half their fields blank. Those are most of the real sessions and none of
the pretty screenshots, so they get designed like they matter, because they do.

And a note on the web-design side that carries all of it: brand and visual
direction, a type scale and photography language that make a directory feel
like a place instead of a spreadsheet, page design across jobs with little in
common (expressive marketing pages, search results that get out of the way,
editorial in between), and responsive behavior that survives a phone in a
parking lot and a desktop at 200% zoom.

## Both reframes have to survive being used twice

Every interface above runs in two places, once on the marketplace and once in
the agent platform. A change to a button is a change in two products. That's
the job the design system does. It's what keeps the two reframes from quietly
breaking the second they leave the screen I designed them on.

- **Tokens, foundations, components.** Foundations are locked. Components are
  still in progress and I'm publishing them as they get done.
- **The accessibility floor lives in the components, not in a checklist.** 18px
  base type, 48 by 48 targets, AA contrast as the minimum. Because it's baked
  into the component, I can't accidentally undo it on page eleven. That's what
  "design from an accessibility floor instead of toward one" actually means in
  practice.
- **<Tech name="storybook" /> is where components get built and argued with**
  before they go anywhere near a page. I can put a component in every state it
  has, at every size, at 200% zoom, and watch it fail on its own instead of
  finding out inside a layout.
- **The two-product test.** Storybook is also how I know a component is ready
  for both the marketplace and the agent platform, because I can look at it
  outside of either one.

## Shipping the front end

I build what I design, which is the part that removes the most steps. The
handoff between design and engineering exists mainly for complex contributions
that connect to the data layer; everything else, I ship.

- Components, layout, responsive behavior and motion all ship as code from me
  instead of as a spec I hand to someone else.
- Accessibility gets verified on the real build, meaning contrast, focus order,
  keyboard paths, zoom and target sizes, because a mockup can't tell you what
  happens at 200 percent.
- Whatever I learn shipping goes back into the system, so the next screen starts
  further along than the last one did.

The part I like most is that I never have to defend any of it in a handoff
meeting. If something needs to change, I go change it. That's the third rule,
keep shipping, and it's less a principle than the thing that makes the other
two hold.

## The stack

| Tool | Layer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Tech name="nextjs" /> | Framework | Server rendering, because a lot of this traffic is a phone on cell service. Typed routes help as the directory grows. |
| <Tech name="react" /> <Tech name="typescript" /> | UI | TypeScript catches the things I'd otherwise find in the browser. |
| <Tech name="tailwindcss" /> | Styling | Tokens live as CSS variables, so a system change is one edit instead of forty. |
| <Tech name="shadcn-ui" /> | Components | I own the source instead of importing it, so when I need to fix an accessibility issue I fix it inside the component. |
| <Tech name="figma" /> | Design | Where foundations and components live. Tokens map one to one with the CSS variables. |
| <Tech name="storybook" /> | Components | Where components get built and tested in isolation, in every state, before they land in either product. |
| <Tech name="vercel" /> | Deployment | A preview build per branch, so I can look at a change on a real phone before it merges. |
| <Tech name="github" /> | Version control | Where the design and the code meet. |
| <Tech name="mobbin" /> | Research | Search and discovery patterns from real estate marketplaces, which are further along on this problem than senior living is. |
| <Tech name="posthog" /> | Analytics | Events and funnels for the search, compare, contact flow, so I can see where families stop. Session replay for the part the numbers only hint at. |
| Spencer's side | Data and infrastructure | Home data, integrations, and everything that has to stay up. |
